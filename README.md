## 📘 Guides: things I wish I knew

Click a guide to open it right here. Each one ends with its own list of free resources.

### Start here (year 1)

1. **Go to class and go to office hours.** Doing your own work in your first CS classes is the base for everything else.
2. **Join your school's ACM chapter or CS club** and go to one meeting ([networking](guides/networking.md)).
3. **Pick one small [project](guides/projects.md)** and build it with a friend.
4. **Put it on [GitHub](guides/github.md)** with a clear README.
5. **Write a one-page [resume](guides/resume.md)** and ask an upperclassman or your career center to look at it.

That's enough for year 1. If you have time left, set up [LinkedIn](guides/linkedin.md) and read the [4-year timeline](guides/timeline.md).

F-1 student? Read the [note for F-1 students](guides/timeline.md#note-for-f-1-students) before you accept any job, even on campus.

### Year 2

Take Data Structures seriously, build one bigger [project](guides/projects.md) with a team, and start [applying](guides/applying.md) for internships in late summer (July to September for the next summer). After Data Structures, start [coding practice](guides/timeline.md#coding-interview-practice).

<summary><b>📝 Your first resume</b></summary>

A resume is a one-page summary of what you've done. Recruiters spend a few seconds on it, so keep it clean and easy to scan. You don't need work experience to make a good one.

### The rules

- **One page.** Always, until you have many years of work.
- **Plain layout.** One column, one font, black text. No photo, icons, skill bars, or tables.
- **Save as PDF.** Name it `FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf`. Copy the text out of the PDF and paste it into a plain text file: if it comes out jumbled, the software that reads resumes will see it jumbled too.
- **Professional email.** Your name, not a nickname.

### Sections, in order

1. **Header:** name, email, phone, city and state, LinkedIn, GitHub.
2. **Education:** school, major, expected graduation, GPA if 3.0 or higher (many applications ask for it anyway), Dean's List or honors, relevant classes.
3. **Projects:** your best 2 or 3. This is the main section when you have no experience.
4. **Experience:** any job counts, including retail, food service, campus jobs, or a family business. It shows you show up.
5. **Leadership and activities:** club roles, competitions ([ICPC](https://icpc.global), hackathons), tutoring, volunteering, events you organized.
6. **Skills:** languages, tools, and frameworks you have really used.

### Bullet formula

Each bullet is: **action word + what you did + how or what came of it.**

- Weak: "Worked on a website."
- Better: "Built a study-planner web app in React and Node.js; 12 classmates used it during finals."
- Better, without a number: "Built a study-planner web app in React and Node.js with login, reminders, and 20 unit tests."
- Weak: "Helped at club events."
- Better: "Organized 6 club meetups for 40+ members; handled booking, signup, and setup."

Good numbers: real users, tests, response time, records handled, people led. **Never invent a number.** Interviewers ask about them. If you can't measure it, describe what it does and how you built or tested it. Lines of code is not a good number.

Start bullets with words like Built, Designed, Wrote, Tested, Organized, Led, Fixed, Reduced. Use 2 to 4 bullets per item.

### No experience? Use this

- **Projects:** class projects (if allowed), personal apps, hackathon builds, team projects.
- **Coursework:** 4 to 6 relevant classes (for example, Data Structures).
- **Clubs and competitions:** members and officers both count.
- **Skills from other jobs:** teamwork, customer service, and handling money are real skills.

Only write true things. Interviewers will ask about every line.

### Tailor it

Read the job posting. If it asks for skills you really have (say, Java and SQL), make sure those exact words appear, and move your most relevant project to the top.

### Tools

- **Google Docs or Word** with a plain one-column template is all you need.
- **Optional:** [Overleaf](https://www.overleaf.com) with the "Jake's Resume" template, a free LaTeX template many CS students use. Only if you're curious about LaTeX.

### Common mistakes

- Typos. Read it out loud, then ask a friend.
- Two pages, or long paragraphs instead of bullets.
- Skills you can't talk about in an interview.
- Missing links to GitHub or projects.

**Get it reviewed** by an upperclassman who has had an internship, and by your career center.

### Resources

- [Tech Interview Handbook: resume guide](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/resume/)
- [Jake's Resume template (Overleaf)](https://www.overleaf.com/latex/templates/jakes-resume/syzfjbzwjncs) (optional, LaTeX)
- [Forage](https://www.theforage.com): free virtual job simulations from big companies, worth a resume line if you have no experience.
- [Extern](https://www.extern.com): remote, project-based "externships" with real companies.
- [Parker Dewey](https://www.parkerdewey.com): short paid "micro-internship" projects.

<summary><b>🛠️ Your first projects</b></summary>

A project is a program you build yourself. It shows you can learn and finish things, and it's the main thing on a first resume.

### How to pick one

- Build something you, your friends, or your club would really use.
- Small enough to finish in 2 to 4 weeks.
- One language you already know a little.
- **Build it with 1 or 2 friends if you can.** Working on shared code (Git branches, splitting tasks, reviewing each other's work) is the skill jobs actually need.

### First project vs. second project

Your **first project** is for learning. Calculators, to-do lists, and quiz games are fine for that, but recruiters have seen thousands of them, so they won't get you an interview on their own.

Your **second project** should solve a real problem for real people. Ideas:

- A tool for your club: event signups, attendance, a Discord bot.
- A tool for a class: a study planner, a practice-question app, a course-schedule helper.
- Something for a local business, a family business, or a nonprofit you know.
- A data project: collect public data (sports, weather, prices) and show something interesting about it.
- Hardware or CE student: an Arduino or Raspberry Pi build, like a sensor logger. Photograph it and put the code on GitHub.

### How to finish and ship

- [ ] Write down 3 features. Build only those.
- [ ] Put the code on GitHub from day one ([GitHub guide](guides/github.md)).
- [ ] Write a README with a screenshot and how to run it.
- [ ] Put it online if it's a website ([GitHub Pages](https://pages.github.com) is free for simple sites). A dead link is worse than no link.
- [ ] Ask two people to try it. Fix what confuses them.
- [ ] Write down one bug you fixed or one thing you measured. That's your interview story.
- [ ] Add it to your [resume](guides/resume.md) with an honest bullet.

A finished small project beats five half-finished big ones.

### Where to learn (free)

Pick one and stick with it:

- **Never coded outside class:** [CS50x](https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/) (Harvard's intro course).
- **Terminal, Git, and tools:** [The Missing Semester](https://missing.csail.mit.edu) (MIT).

Two evenings a week is plenty. Your classes come first.

<summary><b>📨 Applying and interviews</b></summary>

For most students, the hard part isn't being good enough. It's applying early, applying enough, and getting a person to look at your resume.

### Timing

- Big-company summer internships open about **July to September of the year before** and often fill by October. Apply in the first week or two after a posting opens.
- Smaller and local companies, government, and research labs hire later, into spring.
- This job board lists new postings daily with the date each company posted them, so check it often in the fall.

### Volume

Expect to send a lot of applications. 50 to 100 in a season is normal, and most will get no answer. That's how it works for almost everyone, not a sign that you're bad.

Track everything in a simple spreadsheet: company, role, date applied, link, referral (yes/no), status. Follow up once if you hear nothing after 2 to 3 weeks and you have a contact there.

### Referrals

A referral means someone at the company submits your name. It's the best way to get a human to read your resume.

- **Who:** alumni from your school, upperclassmen who interned there, people you met at fairs or events.
- **How:** have a real conversation first. Then ask: "I'm applying to [role, link]. Would you be comfortable referring me? Here's my resume." Make it easy for them.
- If they say no or don't answer, thank them and move on.

### Online assessments (OAs)

Many companies send a timed coding test ([HackerRank](https://www.hackerrank.com), [CodeSignal](https://codesignal.com), or similar) right after you apply, often before a person looks at your resume.

- 60 to 90 minutes, 2 to 4 problems.
- Practice timed problems ([coding practice](guides/timeline.md#coding-interview-practice)).
- Do it in a quiet place, in a language you know well.
- Don't cheat or use AI tools. Companies check, and they will ask about your code later.

### Interviews

**Recruiter call (15 to 30 minutes):** why this company, what you've built, when you graduate, work authorization. Have a 30-second answer to "tell me about yourself."

**Technical interview:** solve a problem while talking through your thinking. Ask questions first, start with a simple solution, then improve it. Test your code with an example.

**Behavioral interview:** questions like "tell me about a time you worked on a team." Answer with **STAR**: the Situation, your Task, the Action you took, and the Result. Prepare 5 stories from projects, jobs, and clubs: a team conflict, a mistake you fixed, something you led, something hard you learned, a deadline you hit.

Practice out loud with a friend, or at your career center's mock interviews.

### Rejections and silence

You will get many. Every person who got an internship also got rejections. After each season, look at where you got stuck:

- **No responses at all:** fix your resume, get referrals, apply earlier.
- **Failing OAs:** more timed practice.
- **Failing interviews:** more mock interviews, better STAR stories.

### Offers

- You usually get 1 to 3 weeks to decide. It's fine to ask for more time politely.
- Once you accept an offer, don't back out for another one. It burns bridges.

### Resources

**Interview prep**
- [Tech Interview Handbook](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org): coding, behavioral, and negotiation guides, plus the [Grind 75](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/grind75/) study plan.
- [NeetCode](https://neetcode.io) and [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com): the NeetCode 150 list, videos, and practice.
- [Kattis](https://open.kattis.com) and [Codeforces](https://codeforces.com): contest-style practice.

**More job lists** (this board doesn't catch everything)
- [Simplify's Summer 2027 internship list](https://github.com/SimplifyJobs/Summer2027-Internships) and [new-grad list](https://github.com/SimplifyJobs/New-Grad-Positions).
- [Handshake](https://joinhandshake.com) (through your school) and LinkedIn Jobs.

**No internship yet?** [Extern](https://www.extern.com), [Forage](https://www.theforage.com), [Parker Dewey](https://www.parkerdewey.com), undergrad research, and campus jobs all count as experience.

<summary><b>🤝 Networking, clubs, and programs</b></summary>

Networking means getting to know people who can teach you, vouch for you, or tell you about openings. At a small school, a few real relationships matter more than hundreds of LinkedIn connections.

### 1. Professors (start here)

- **Go to office hours,** even with a small question. Professors write recommendation letters, know about research spots, and hear about jobs.
- **Ask about research.** "Are you working on anything a student could help with? I'm happy to start with small tasks." Many undergrad research jobs are never posted.
- **Summer research (REUs):** the National Science Foundation funds paid summer research programs for undergrads at universities across the country. Search "[NSF REU sites](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/reu)". Most deadlines are January to March. Freshmen and sophomores do get in.

### 2. Upperclassmen

The most useful people you can know are juniors and seniors who already had internships. Ask one to look at your resume, tell you which classes are hard, and tell you how they got their first internship. Most will say yes. Later, do the same for the people behind you.

### 3. Clubs, teams, and leadership

- **ACM chapter or CS club.** Go to a meeting this month. Once you've been around for a semester, volunteer to run one thing: a workshop, a study session, an event. A year later, run for an officer role. Leading something real gives you the best answers to "tell me about a time you..." interview questions.
- **[ICPC](https://icpc.global) and programming contests.** If your school sends teams to the [ICPC](https://icpc.global) (a team programming contest), join the practice sessions. It's the best coding interview practice there is, and it goes on your resume.
- **Hackathons.** Weekend events where teams build something fast. Beginners are welcome, many are free, and some are online. For your first one: go with a friend or join a team there, finish something small, and ignore the prizes. Find them on [Major League Hacking (MLH)](https://mlh.com) and [Devpost](https://devpost.com).
- **Other clubs.** A cybersecurity, robotics, or engineering club, if your school has one.

### 4. Campus jobs

Tutoring, lab assistant, IT help desk, or grading for a professor. They pay, they're flexible, they go on your resume, and they put you next to faculty. (F-1 students: on-campus work has hour limits. Ask your DSO first.)

### Career fairs

- [ ] Bring a printed resume. Look up 3 companies first.
- [ ] Say: "Hi, I'm [name], a [year] in CS. I'm interested in [role]. What do you look for in interns?"
- [ ] Ask for the rep's name and email, then connect or send a thank-you that day, and mention you applied.

Big companies' reps often just say "apply online." That's normal. **Local and regional employers** (energy, healthcare, banks, government, manufacturing) often hire straight from your fair, and they're great first internships.

### Programs and communities

Read the official pages; dates and rules change every year.

- **Early internships for first-years and sophomores** ([Google STEP](https://www.google.com/about/careers/applications/students/), [Explore Microsoft](https://careers.microsoft.com/v2/global/en/exploremicrosoft), and similar programs at banks and tech companies). Very competitive, so treat them as long shots. Apply, but don't count on them.
- **[CodePath](https://www.codepath.org):** free courses for college CS students.
- **Communities:** [ColorStack](https://www.colorstack.org) (Black and Latinx students in computing), [SWE](https://swe.org) (women in engineering), [NSBE](https://www.nsbe.org), [SHPE](https://www.shpe.org), and others. Many give free conference trips and job connections.
- **Conferences:** [Grace Hopper Celebration](https://ghc.anitab.org) and the [Tapia Conference](https://tapiaconference.cmd-it.org) offer travel scholarships. Apply early.

### Reaching out to strangers

Warm contacts (professors, upperclassmen, alumni, people you met) are far better. If you do email a stranger, keep it under 100 words and ask one specific question:

> Subject: [School] CS student, quick question
>
> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] CS student at [School]. I read about your work on [specific thing] and I'm learning [topic]. Could I ask: [one specific question]?
>
> Thank you,
> [Name] · [LinkedIn]

Expect fewer than 1 in 10 to reply. That's normal, not personal.

### Follow up

- Send a thank-you within 24 hours.
- A month later, tell them what you did with their advice.
- Keep a simple list: name, date, what you talked about.

### Resources

**Communities and organizations.** Join one or two; many give free conference trips, mentors, and recruiter connections.
- [ACM](https://www.acm.org): student chapters, contests, and conferences for everyone in computing.
- [ColorStack](https://www.colorstack.org): Black and Latinx CS students.
- [NSBE](https://www.nsbe.org): Black engineers and CS students; big national convention and career fair.
- [SHPE](https://www.shpe.org): Hispanic engineers and CS students; big national convention and career fair.
- [SWE](https://swe.org): women in engineering and CS.
- [Rewriting the Code](https://rewritingthecode.org): women in college studying tech.
- [AnitaB.org](https://anitab.org): women in tech; runs the [Grace Hopper Celebration](https://ghc.anitab.org).
- [Out in Tech](https://outintech.com) and [oSTEM](https://ostem.org): LGBTQ+ people in tech and STEM.
- [AISES](https://www.aises.org): Indigenous students in STEM.
- (Women Who Code closed in 2024. [Rewriting the Code](https://rewritingthecode.org) and [AnitaB.org](https://anitab.org) are good alternatives.)

**Programs**
- [CodePath](https://www.codepath.org): free courses for college students (interview prep, web, mobile, AI), with company connections.

**Hackathons and contests**

<summary><b>🗓️ 4-year timeline (and F-1 rules)</b></summary>

A map, not a rule. If you're behind, start where you are today. If you're taking a heavy course load or working a job, do less. That's fine.

**The big date to know:** summer internships at big companies open about **July to September of the year before**, and many fill up by October. "Summer 2028 internship" means applying in fall 2027.

### Freshman year

**Fall**
- [ ] Go to class, do your own work, go to office hours.
- [ ] Join your ACM chapter or CS club.
- [ ] Early programs for first-years mostly close in the fall. Apply to one or two if you like, but they're long shots ([networking](guides/networking.md)).

**Spring**
- [ ] Write your first [resume](guides/resume.md).
- [ ] Build one small [project](guides/projects.md), ideally with a friend.
- [ ] Apply to summer research programs (REUs) and campus jobs.

**Summer**
- Any job is a good summer. If you have spare time, build a project or take a free course.

### Sophomore year

**Fall**
- [ ] Take Data Structures seriously. It's the class interviews are built on.
- [ ] Apply to sophomore programs and internships (see [applying](guides/applying.md)).
- [ ] Go to the career fair. Talk to local employers too.
- [ ] Volunteer to run one thing in a club.

**Spring**
- [ ] Keep applying. Smaller and local companies hire into spring.
- [ ] Ask a professor about research.
- [ ] Build a bigger project with a team.

**Summer** (before junior year)
- [ ] Coding practice: 4 or 5 problems a week for 8 to 10 weeks before fall.
- [ ] Polish your resume and apply the day postings open.

### Junior year

- **Fall:** Apply in the first two weeks of postings (July to September). This internship often leads to a full-time offer.
- **Spring:** Interviews and offers. If nothing comes through, plan a summer of research, a serious project, or a local job. That still counts.

### Senior year

- **Fall:** Apply for full-time jobs (they open in late summer), or accept a return offer. Thinking about grad school? Ask professors for letters early in the fall.
- **Spring:** Decide, finish your degree, and thank the people who helped. Then help the students behind you.

### Coding interview practice

Most internship applications include an online coding test or interview.

- Start after you've taken Data Structures (or while taking it).
- Learn the patterns: arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, recursion, trees, graphs.
- Use one language you know well (Java, Python, or C++).
- Follow [NeetCode](https://neetcode.io)'s list of 150 problems and practice them on [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com).
- Time yourself, and practice explaining out loud as you code.
- [ICPC](https://icpc.global) practice and contests count as practice too.

### Note for F-1 students

Rules depend on your situation, so check everything with your DSO (Designated School Official, the person at your school who handles F-1 rules).

- **On-campus jobs** are allowed with hour limits during the semester. Ask your DSO first.
- **CPT** (Curricular Practical Training) lets you work off campus in your field while you study. It usually needs one full academic year of study first and is often tied to a course or credit. Your DSO must approve it, for that specific employer and dates, **before** you start. Never start early.
- **OPT** (Optional Practical Training) is usually how you work after graduation (up to 12 months, plus a 24-month STEM extension with eligible employers). Ask your DSO about it before your last year.
- **Read postings carefully.** Skip ones that say "no CPT/OPT" (the job board marks these 🚫) or "US citizens only" (🇺🇸). "We don't sponsor visas" is not the same thing: some of those companies still hire interns on CPT. Ask the recruiter.
- **Application forms** ask "Are you authorized to work in the US?" and "Will you need sponsorship now or in the future?" Answer truthfully every time, and ask your DSO how to answer for your case.
- Apply widely. Smaller companies, universities, and research labs are often more open to international students.

### Resources

- [F-1 CPT, official DHS page](https://studyinthestates.dhs.gov/sevis-help-hub/student-records/fm-student-employment/f-1-curricular-practical-training-cpt)
- [NeetCode](https://neetcode.io) (free problem list and videos) and [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com) (practice problems)
- [Tech Interview Handbook: Grind 75](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/grind75/): a week-by-week study plan.

<summary><b>🐙 GitHub setup</b></summary>

GitHub is where programmers store and share code. Few recruiters look at it during the first screen. It matters later: when a hiring manager or interviewer clicks your link, it shows that your resume is true. One finished repo with a good README is enough to start.

### Make your account

- [ ] Sign up at github.com with a personal email (your school email can stop working after you graduate).
- [ ] Pick a simple username, like your name. Avoid jokes and numbers.
- [ ] Add a clear photo and your name.
- [ ] Turn on two-factor login in Settings.

### Profile and pins

- Optional: a profile README (a public repo with the **same name as your username**) with 3 lines: who you are, what you're learning, and your LinkedIn link. Skip badges and stat widgets.
- Pin 1 to 3 repos that actually run and have a README. Don't pin empty homework folders.

### What a good repo looks like

- [ ] A clear name (`study-planner`, not `project1`).
- [ ] A README with: what it does (one sentence), a screenshot, how to run it, and what you used.
- [ ] It runs with one or two commands, or has a live link that works.
- [ ] No passwords or API keys in the code. Ever.
- [ ] A `.gitignore` file so you don't upload junk files.
- [ ] Commit messages that say what you did ("Add login form", not "stuff").

### GitHub Student Developer Pack

Free tools and trials for students (cloud credits, domains, developer tools). Apply with your school email or proof of enrollment. It can take a few days.

### Good habits

- Put class projects on GitHub only if your professor allows it. Never post exam answers or code you were told to keep private.
- Read other people's code.
- When you're ready for open source, pick a project you actually use and look for issues labeled "good first issue".

### Resources

- [GitHub Student Developer Pack](https://education.github.com/pack): free tools, cloud credits, and domains.
- [GitHub Skills](https://skills.github.com): free hands-on courses inside GitHub.
- [Pro Git](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2): the free official Git book.
- [Make a README](https://www.makeareadme.com): a simple guide with a template.
- [First Contributions](https://github.com/firstcontributions/first-contributions): practice making your first pull request.
- [Good First Issue](https://goodfirstissue.dev): beginner-friendly issues in real open-source projects.
- [JetBrains student licenses](https://www.jetbrains.com/community/education/#students): free IntelliJ, PyCharm, CLion, and more.

<details>
<summary><b>💼 LinkedIn setup</b></summary>

LinkedIn is where professionals and recruiters find each other. For a freshman it matters less than your classes and projects, so spend about an hour setting it up and then keep it updated.

### Profile checklist

- [ ] **Photo.** Face in good light, plain background, shoulders up. A phone photo is fine.
- [ ] **Name.** The same name as on your resume.
- [ ] **Location.** Your city, or the city you want to work in.
- [ ] **Custom URL.** Change it to `linkedin.com/in/yourname` in settings.

### Headline

The line under your name. Without one, LinkedIn shows only "Student".

> [Year] [Major] Student at [School] | [Skill] | [Skill]

Optional: add "Seeking Summer 20XX Internship" once you're actually applying.

### About section

3 or 4 short sentences: who you are (year, major), what you're learning or building, what you're looking for, and your email.

### Fill in the rest

- **Education:** school, major, expected graduation date.
- **Experience:** any job counts, even non-tech. One or two bullets each.
- **Projects:** each with a one-line description and the GitHub link.
- **Skills:** 5 to 10 you have really used.
- **Clubs and leadership:** ACM, other clubs, events you organized.
- **Languages:** list them if you speak more than one.

### Connecting

Connect with people you actually know or have talked to: classmates, club members, professors, people you met at events or career fairs (send the request the same day).

**Alumni from your school are your best contacts.** Search your school's page, then "People", and filter by company. Ask a professor or club officer if they know alumni in tech.

Note template (keep it short):

> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] CS student at [School] and saw you studied there too. I'm interested in [field]. Could I ask you one quick question about how you got started at [Company]?

Ask one specific question first. If they reply, then ask for a 15-minute chat. Many people won't answer; that's normal.

After you apply to a company, it's fine to message the recruiter or an alum there with one line saying you applied and why you're interested.

### Resources

- Your school's alumni: go to your school's LinkedIn page and click "Alumni" (or "People") to filter by company, city, and what they studied.
- [LinkedIn guide, University of Chicago Career Advancement](https://careeradvancement.uchicago.edu/career-toolkit/interviewing-and-networking/linkedin-guide/)
- [LinkedIn guide, Colorado State University Career Center](https://career.colostate.edu/linkedin/)

## New to internships? Start here

**📘 [Student guides](guides/README.md):** short how-tos for CS and CE students on GitHub, LinkedIn, your first resume, projects, networking, applying and interviews, and a 4-year timeline. **[Resources](guides/resources.md):** communities, programs, free courses, hackathons, and tools.

- **Apply early.** Most summer internships open between August and October of the year before, and many fill by winter.
- **Freshmen and sophomores:** start with 🌱 Early Programs. In the other lists, open the posting and check the qualifications for class year and GPA.
- **Internship vs. co-op:** an internship is usually a summer. A co-op is usually a full semester or more of full-time work, often with a break from classes.
- **Don't wait to meet every bullet.** If you meet most of the qualifications, apply.
- **Get your resume checked** by your career center or an upperclassman who has had an internship before you send it out.
