# 4-Year Timeline

A map, not a rule. If you're behind, start where you are today. If you're taking a heavy course load or working a job, do less. That's fine.

> **Started late or transferred?** Don't read this as a list of what you missed. Find your row in [Where are you?](README.md#where-are-you), then use the [6-week sprint](applying.md#behind-start-here). Come back here for the dates.

**The big date to know:** summer internships at big companies open about **July to September of the year before**, and many fill up by the fall. "Summer 2028 internship" means applying in fall 2027.

That is the early path, not the only one. Many roles are rolling, which means the company reviews applications as they arrive and hires until the spots are full. Some stay open into winter. Local employers often hire into spring. If you missed the fall, keep applying.

**Your first internship target:** any internship by the end of sophomore year, even a small or local one. Then climb: local, then mid-size, then big. Each one makes the next easier.

## Freshman year

**Fall**
- [ ] Go to class, do your own work, go to office hours.
- [ ] Join your ACM chapter or CS club.
- [ ] Early programs for first-years mostly close in the fall. Apply to one or two if you like, but they're long shots ([networking](networking.md)).

**Spring**
- [ ] Write your first [resume](resume.md).
- [ ] Build one small [project](projects.md), ideally with a friend.
- [ ] Apply to summer research programs (REUs) and campus jobs. REU deadlines are usually January to March, so look in the fall.
- [ ] Easy coding start: solve one easy problem a week in the language from your class.

**Summer**
- Any job is a good summer (international students: check your work rules first). If you have spare time, build a project or take a free course.

**Done by the end of freshman year**

| Step | Done looks like |
|---|---|
| Go to class and office hours | A professor or TA knows your name, and you've asked at least three questions in office hours. |
| Join a CS club ([networking](networking.md)) | You've been to three meetings and know two upperclassmen well enough to ask a question. |
| Build one small [project](projects.md) | A repo a stranger can run by following the README, plus a one-line result ("Used by 12 classmates to split rent"). |
| Put it on [GitHub](github.md) | Your profile has your real name, and the project is pinned with a clear README. |
| Write a [resume](resume.md) | One page, saved as a PDF, reviewed by at least one person (career center, upperclassman, or professor). |
| Start your [wins file](README.md#start-a-wins-file) | One doc with every project, job, and result so far. |
| First summer | Any job counts, and it goes on your resume. Research or a finished project is great. |

## Sophomore year

**Fall**
- [ ] Take Data Structures seriously. It's the class interviews are built on.
- [ ] Apply to sophomore programs and internships as early as you can (see [applying](applying.md)).
- [ ] Go to the career fair. Talk to local employers too.
- [ ] Volunteer to run one thing in a club.

**Spring**
- [ ] Keep applying. Smaller and local companies hire into spring, so this is your best window if you started late.
- [ ] Ask a professor about research.
- [ ] Build a bigger project with a team.
- [ ] Coding practice: 2 or 3 problems a week.

**Summer** (before junior year)
- [ ] Build up to about 5 timed problems a week (see below).
- [ ] Polish your resume and apply the week postings open.

**Done by the end of sophomore year**

| Step | Done looks like |
|---|---|
| Data Structures | You can explain and code a hash map lookup, a stack, and a breadth-first search without notes. |
| Bigger team [project](projects.md) | Two or more people committed code, it runs or is live, and you can name one number (users, speed, data size). |
| Coding practice ([plan](#coding-interview-practice)) | 2-3 problems a week, every week, from one list. |
| [Apply](applying.md) | Applications tracked in a spreadsheet, including local and mid-size companies, plus one career fair where you talked to five employers. |
| [LinkedIn](linkedin.md) | Profile matches your resume, with a photo and your project links. |
| Research or campus job | You asked one professor about research, or you hold a campus job you can describe in one bullet. |
| Second summer | An internship of any size, or research, or a job plus one finished project. |

## Junior year

- **Fall:** Apply as early as you can, from July on. Roles at big companies fill, but many stay open for months. This internship often leads to a full-time offer.
- **Spring:** Interviews and offers. If nothing comes through, plan a summer of research, a serious project, or a local job. That still counts, and you can apply again in the fall.
- **Government, defense, and labs:** these often open in the fall for the next summer, because background checks and clearances take months. Don't wait until spring. See [tracks: government and defense](tracks.md#government-and-defense).

## Senior year

- **Fall:** Apply for full-time jobs (many open in late summer), or accept a return offer. Thinking about grad school? Ask professors for letters early in the fall.
- **Spring:** Smaller companies are still hiring. Keep applying until you sign.
- **Offer deadlines:** an offer comes with a reply-by date. If you need more time, ask in writing and say why. See [offers](applying.md#offers).
- **Finish:** Decide, finish your degree, and thank the people who helped. Then help the students behind you.

## Coding interview practice

Most software internship applications include an online coding test or interview. Start light and build up. Pacing:

| When | Problems a week |
|---|---|
| Freshman year | 1 easy one a week, to get used to it |
| Sophomore year (after or during Data Structures) | 2 or 3 |
| The 8 to 10 weeks before the fall you apply | Build to about 5, with a timer |

How to do it:
- Learn the patterns: arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, recursion, trees, graphs.
- Use one language you know well (Java, Python, or C++).
- Pick **one** list and finish it, such as [NeetCode](https://neetcode.io)'s 150 problems, and practice on [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com).
- Set a 30-minute timer. If you're stuck at 20 minutes, read the solution, then redo the problem from scratch the next day.
- Practice explaining out loud as you code.
- [ICPC](https://icpc.global) practice and contests count as practice too.

This plan is for software roles. Hardware, embedded, and security interviews ask different things, so see [tracks](tracks.md) before you spend weeks on LeetCode.

## Return offers

A return offer is an invitation to come back, for another internship or full-time. Most of the work happens during the internship. Do these:

- [ ] **Week 1:** Ask your manager, "What does it take to get a return offer here?" Write down the answer.
- [ ] **Midpoint:** Ask for feedback. "What should I do more of, and what should I fix?" Then act on it.
- [ ] **Ship one scoped thing.** Agree on a small piece of work you can finish, finish it, and demo it to the team.
- [ ] **Keep a list** of what you shipped, with numbers where you can, in your [wins file](README.md#start-a-wins-file).
- [ ] **Before your last week:** Ask directly, "I'd love to come back. Is that possible, and what are the next steps?"

If there's no offer, ask for feedback and a reference, and keep in touch. Many people get offers from their second try.

**Co-ops.** A co-op is a longer work term, often a full semester, and some schools have a formal co-op program that you sign up for through the career center. Co-ops can repeat, so you may work for the same company more than once. Ask your career center how co-ops work at your school and how they affect your graduation date.

## Note for F-1 students

> This is general information, not legal or immigration advice. Rules change and depend on your situation, so confirm everything with your DSO (Designated School Official, the person at your school who handles F-1 rules) or an immigration attorney.

- **On-campus jobs** are generally limited to 20 hours a week while classes are in session. Ask your DSO first.
- **CPT** (Curricular Practical Training) lets you work off campus in your field while you study. You usually need a full academic year of full-time study first. Each CPT approval covers one employer and set dates, and your DSO must approve it in the system **before** you start. Never start early.
- **Full-time CPT is more than 20 hours a week.** A year or more of full-time CPT makes you ineligible for OPT.
- **OPT** (Optional Practical Training) is usually how you work after graduation, for up to 12 months. Some STEM degrees can get a 24-month extension, but only with an employer that uses E-Verify and a written training plan. OPT has limits on unemployed days, and you must report your work. Unpaid work counts too. Ask your DSO about the timing well before your last semester.
- **Rules are changing.** A 2026 federal rule replaced "duration of status" with fixed admission periods. Ask your DSO what it means for your dates.
- **Read postings carefully.** Skip ones that say "no CPT/OPT" (the job board marks these 🚫) or "US citizens only" (🇺🇸). "We don't sponsor visas" is not the same thing: some of those companies still hire interns on CPT. Ask the recruiter.
- **Application forms** ask "Are you authorized to work in the US?" and "Will you need sponsorship now or in the future?" Answer truthfully every time, and ask your DSO how to answer for your case.
- **Many research programs** (such as most NSF REUs) are open to citizens and permanent residents only. Check each program's rules before you spend time on it.
- Apply widely. Smaller companies, universities, and research labs are often more open to international students.
