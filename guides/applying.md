# Applying

For most students, the hard part isn't being good enough. It's applying early, applying in the right places, and getting a person to read your resume. This guide takes you from a finished [resume](resume.md) to interviews. For the interviews themselves, see [interviews](interviews.md).

## Timing

- **Earlier is better.** Many big-company summer internships open **July to September of the year before** and fill on a rolling basis, so the first applicants get the first interview slots.
- **Late is not too late.** Plenty of big-company postings stay open into winter. Local employers, government contractors, and startups hire into spring, some as late as April or May.
- New postings show up on this [job board](../INTERNSHIPS.md) daily with the date each company posted them. Check it a few times a week in the fall and about once a week the rest of the year.

## Where to apply

If you're not at a famous school, don't spend all your effort where 5,000 people apply. Apply where 50 people apply.

1. **Your school's career fair and Handshake.** Employers there came looking for students from your school. This is the main channel for most students. Go every semester, even as a freshman.
2. **Regional employers.** Banks, insurance companies, energy and utility companies, hospitals and healthcare systems, manufacturers, and government and defense contractors all hire CS and CE interns. Many never post on the big internship lists.
3. **Mid-size companies and startups.** Fewer applicants, and you often do real work.
4. **Big tech.** Apply, but don't make it your whole plan.
5. **This [job board](../INTERNSHIPS.md)**, plus the other lists under [Resources](#resources).

A first internship anywhere makes the next one much easier. Local, then mid-size, then big is a normal path.

## How many to send

- **Big-company searches often take 100 or more applications.** Most get no answer. That alone doesn't mean you're bad.
- **Referrals and local employers do far better per application.** Ten applications with referrals can beat 100 cold ones.
- **Tailor only your top 10 to 15 targets** (adjust your resume's keywords and project order to the posting). Send a solid general version to everyone else. Volume matters more than polish after the first version is good.
- If you've sent 40 or more and almost nobody replies, stop sending and run the [funnel check](#funnel-check).

## Weekly routine in season

Pick fixed blocks and protect them, like a class. One example for a student with about 10 free hours a week:

| Block | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| Apply | 2 to 3 hours | Send applications, update your tracker |
| Practice and build | 6 to 7 hours | [Coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice), your [project](projects.md) |
| Follow up | 30 minutes | Thank-you notes, referral follow-ups |

**Order inside the apply block:**

1. **Referral requests first.** Ask before you apply, so the referral can be attached.
2. **Hard deadlines next.** Anything closing this week.
3. **Everything else**, newest postings first.

**Late in the season** (January on), sweep older postings that are still open. Many are, and fewer people apply to them.

## Tracker and answer sheet

**Tracker.** One spreadsheet row per application:

| Company | Role | Link | Date applied | Referral | Status | OA date | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example Bank | Software Intern | (link) | Sep 12 | Yes, alum | OA sent | Sep 18 | Do OA by Sep 18 |

Status values: applied, OA, interview, offer, rejected, no reply. After 3 weeks with no reply, mark it "no reply" and move on.

**Answer sheet.** One document you copy from so each form takes minutes, not an hour:

- Full name, email, phone, city and state
- School, degree, major, expected graduation (month and year), GPA if 3.0 or higher
- LinkedIn, GitHub, and portfolio links
- Work authorization answers (they're the same every time; answer truthfully)
- Short answers you reuse and lightly edit: "Why this company?" (2 sentences), "Tell us about a project" (3 to 4 sentences), "Describe a challenge you overcame" (one STAR story, see [interviews](interviews.md#behavioral))

Autofill tools are fine for repeated fields. Read every application before you click submit.

## Referrals

A referral means someone who works at the company submits your name. It's the best way to get a human to read your resume.

**What it does:** moves your resume out of the pile and usually gets it a real look. **What it doesn't do:** skip the coding test or the interviews. It won't save a weak resume either.

**Who to ask:** alumni of your school, upperclassmen who interned there, people you met at career fairs, club events, or hackathons. See [networking](networking.md) for how to find alumni.

**When:** before you apply, ideally a week or two before. Many referral systems only work if your application isn't in yet.

**Script for an alum you've never met** (LinkedIn message or email):

> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] studying computer science at [your school], and I saw you're a [role] at [Company]. I'm applying to the [role title] internship ([link]) and I'd really like to work on [team or product, one specific thing]. I've built [one-line project with a result]. Would you be open to a 15-minute call, or to referring me if you think I'm a fit? My resume is attached. Thanks either way.

Make it easy: include the job link, your resume, and two lines on why you fit. If they say no or don't answer after one follow-up a week later, thank them and move on. Never ask someone to refer you to a role you haven't read.

## Funnel check

Applying is a funnel: applications, then replies, then online assessments (OAs), then interviews, then offers. Find the first stage where you're stuck and fix only that one. Fixing a later stage doesn't help if you never reach it.

| What you see | Most likely problem | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Under about 5% replies after 40+ applications | Resume or targeting | Run the [resume audit](resume.md#resume-audit), get a human to review it, apply to more regional and mid-size employers, get referrals |
| You get OAs but no interviews | OA speed and accuracy | Timed [coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice), 4 to 5 problems a week, under a timer |
| You get interviews but no offers | Interview skills | Practice out loud with [interviews](interviews.md), do mock interviews at your career center |

Run the check after every 40 applications or so, not after every rejection.

## Behind? Start here

For a junior, transfer, or anyone starting late with no internship, no projects, and little on the resume. Six weeks, about 10 to 12 hours a week. If you work a job, stretch it to 10 weeks.

| Week | Do this | Done looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write or fix your [resume](resume.md). Get it reviewed by your career center or an upperclassman. Start your tracker and answer sheet. | A one-page resume someone else has read |
| 2 | Pick one [project](projects.md) that real people will use (a club, a family business, classmates). Start building. Start daily practice: one easy coding problem a day. | A repo with a README and a first working piece |
| 3 | Send 10 targeted applications, mostly local and mid-size employers. Ask 3 people for referrals. Go to any career fair or employer event. | 10 rows in your tracker, 3 referral requests sent |
| 4 | Ship a first version of the project to its users. Another 10 applications and 3 referral asks. | Someone other than you has used it |
| 5 | Add the project to your resume with a result ("used by 40 club members"). 10 more applications. One mock interview. | Updated resume, 30+ applications total |
| 6 | 10 more applications. Run the [funnel check](#funnel-check). | A clear next fix, and a plan for the next 6 weeks |

**Widen what counts:**

- **Spring, fall, and off-cycle internships.** Fewer applicants. Some schools let you take a semester off for these.
- **Co-ops.** Paid, longer (often a semester plus a summer), and common at manufacturers, utilities, and defense companies.
- **Part-time roles** during the school year, including IT help desk and campus tech jobs.
- **Undergrad research** with a professor at your school.
- **Local employers** that never post on national lists. Look them up by industry in your city and check their careers pages directly.

## Online assessments

Many companies send a timed coding test right after you apply, often before a person looks at your resume.

- **Format:** usually 60 to 90 minutes, 2 to 4 problems, on a platform like [HackerRank](https://www.hackerrank.com) or [CodeSignal](https://codesignal.com). Hidden test cases check your answer. Some add multiple-choice or debugging questions.
- **How to practice:** timed problems from the [coding practice](timeline.md#coding-interview-practice) list. Try each platform's free practice tests so the editor isn't new on test day. Read the whole problem and the examples before typing.
- **On the day:** quiet room, stable internet, a language you know well. Solve the easiest problem first. A brute-force solution that passes some tests beats a clever one that passes none.
- **AI tools:** read each company's rules in the OA instructions. Most OAs ban AI help, and some record your screen or webcam. Never submit code you can't explain. Companies often ask about your OA code in the next round.

## Offers

- **Reply within a day.** Thank them and say when you'll decide, even if you need time.
- **Get the deadline in writing.** Ask: "Could you confirm the deadline for my decision by email?"
- **Ask for more time politely if you need it:**
  > Thank you so much for the offer. I'm very excited about it. I'm finishing a couple of other interview processes and would like to make a careful decision. Would it be possible to extend my deadline to [date]?
- **Use an offer to speed up others.** Tell companies you're still interviewing with: "I've received an offer with a deadline of [date]. [Company] is my top choice. Is there any way to speed up my process?"
- **Reneging** (accepting an offer, then backing out for another) is rare and burns bridges with that company and sometimes your career center. Don't accept until you're sure.
- **Pay:** intern pay is usually set by the company and rarely negotiated. It's fine to ask once about relocation or housing help. Full-time offers have more room.

## Job-scam red flags

Scammers target students with fake internships and remote jobs. Stop if you see any of these:

- **You're asked to pay** for anything: training, a starter kit, equipment, a background check.
- **They send a check** to buy equipment or set up a home office, then ask you to send some money back. The check is fake and will bounce.
- **Interviews happen only by text or a chat app**, with no video or phone call.
- **No company email.** The "recruiter" writes from a personal email address, and the company's real careers site doesn't list the job.
- **Pay that's too good** for little work, or an offer with no interview at all.
- **They ask for your Social Security number or bank details** before a real, written offer.

If you're unsure, find the company's careers page yourself (not through their link) and check that the job is there. Report scams at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

## Rejections

You will get many. Every person who got an internship also got rejections.

- **It's normal.** Most rejections are about timing and volume, not you.
- **Ask once for feedback** after an interview: "Thank you for letting me know. If you have any feedback that would help me improve, I'd appreciate it." Many companies can't share any. Don't ask again.
- **Log what stumped you.** Keep a list: the OA problem you couldn't finish, the question you fumbled. Practice those next. That list is your study plan.
