# Networking and Getting Involved

Networking means getting to know people who can teach you, vouch for you, or tell you about openings. A few real relationships beat hundreds of LinkedIn connections. You do not need to be outgoing. You need to ask small, specific questions.

If you have about 5 hours a week or less, jump to the [low-time version](#low-time-version).

## Your first 3 weeks

You don't know anyone yet. Start here. Each step takes under an hour.

**Week 1: professors.**
- [ ] Go to office hours once. Use the script below.
- [ ] Find your school's CS club or ACM chapter (ask your department, or check your school's club list). Go to one meeting.

**Week 2: upperclassmen.**
- [ ] Ask one junior or senior for 15 minutes. Use the script below.
- [ ] Ask them: "Is there anyone else I should talk to?"

**Week 3: alumni.**
- [ ] Find 3 alumni and send the short message below. Expect 1 reply or none.
- [ ] Write down everyone you talked to: name, date, what they said, what you'll do next.

**Done looks like:** 1 professor, 1 upperclassman, and 3 alumni messages sent, plus a notes list with 5 names.

## Professors

Professors write recommendation letters, know about research spots, and hear about jobs before they're posted.

**Office-hours script.** Bring a real question from class, then ask:

> Thanks for explaining that. Can I ask a different question? I'm trying to get my first CS experience. Are there any projects, research, or lab work a student could help with? I can offer about 5 hours a week, and I'm happy to start with small tasks like testing, cleaning data, or reading papers.

If the answer is "not right now," ask: "Is there another professor or a lab I should talk to?"

**Before you go,** read one page of the professor's research on the department site. Mention one thing you found interesting. This takes ten minutes and sets you apart.

**Research for pay or credit.** Many undergraduate research jobs are never posted. Ask. Also ask your department whether research can count for credit.

**Summer research (REUs).** The National Science Foundation funds Research Experiences for Undergraduates (REUs): paid summer research at universities around the country, usually with a stipend and often help with housing and travel. Deadlines are usually January to March, and each site sets its own. Eligibility varies by site (some require U.S. citizenship or permanent residency, some don't), so read each listing. First-years and sophomores do get in. Search the [NSF REU site list](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/reu). Your own school's CS or engineering department may run one too.

## Upperclassmen

The most useful people you can know are juniors and seniors who already had internships.

> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] CS student. I saw you interned at [Company] (or: you're in the CS club). Could I ask you 3 quick questions? How did you get your first internship, which classes were hardest, and would you look at my resume for 5 minutes? I'm happy to buy you a coffee or do it over a call.

Most will say yes. Later, do the same for the people behind you. That's how this works.

## Alumni

Alumni are friendly to students from their own school. Steps:

1. Search your school's page on LinkedIn, open **Alumni**, and filter by company, city, or what they studied. See [LinkedIn: finding alumni](linkedin.md#find-alumni).
2. Pick people who graduated in the last 3 to 8 years. They remember what you're going through.
3. Send one short message (under 100 words, one question):

> Hi [Name], I'm a [year] CS student at [School]. I saw you went there too and now work at [Company] as a [role]. I'm trying to learn how people get into [field]. Could I ask you one question: [specific question, like "what did you do before your first internship that mattered most?"]? Thanks either way.

4. If they reply, ask for 15 minutes. Come with 3 questions.
5. Expect about 1 reply for every 5 to 10 messages. That's normal, not personal.

**Follow-up template** (send within 24 hours of any chat):

> Thank you for your time today. Your tip about [specific thing] was helpful. I'm going to [one thing you'll do]. I'll let you know how it goes.

A month later, send two lines on what you did. This is what turns one chat into a contact who thinks of you when a role opens. If you want a referral, see [applying](applying.md#referrals). Don't ask in the first message.

## Clubs and leadership

- **ACM chapter or CS club.** Go to a meeting this month. After a semester, volunteer to run one thing: a workshop, a study session, a speaker night.
- **Running an event or fundraiser counts.** Planning a fundraiser, with a venue, a budget, and asking local businesses to sponsor, is real leadership and negotiation. Put it on your resume with numbers (money raised, people who came). It also makes a strong answer to "tell me about a time you led a team" in [interviews](interviews.md#behavioral).
- **Other clubs.** A cybersecurity, robotics, or engineering club (IEEE student branch, for example) if your school has one. See [tracks](tracks.md) for which fit which career.

## Contests and hackathons

- **[ICPC](https://icpc.global)** is a team programming contest (teams of three, one computer, timed). If your school sends teams, join the practice sessions. It's good practice for solving problems under time pressure, which is what online assessments and technical interviews are. You don't need to win. Going on your resume is a plus, and the practice is the point.
- **Hackathons** are weekend events where teams build something fast. Solo is fine, and many let you join a team at the start. Finish something small, and ignore the prizes. Find them on [Major League Hacking (MLH)](https://mlh.com) and [Devpost](https://devpost.com). Many are free; some are online.

## Career fairs

If you've never been, here's the whole plan.

- [ ] Before: check the company list. Pick 3 you'd be glad to work for. Look up what they do in two minutes each. Add 2 local or regional employers.
- [ ] Bring 5 to 10 printed copies of your resume, even if the fair says "apply online." Dress one step nicer than class.
- [ ] Say: "Hi, I'm [name], a [year] in CS. I'm interested in [role]. What do you look for in interns?"
- [ ] Ask the rep's name and email or LinkedIn. Message them the same day: "Thanks for talking with me. I applied to [role] today."
- [ ] If you freeze up, start with the smaller employers. Practice on them.

Big companies' reps often say "apply online." That's normal. **Local and regional employers** (energy, healthcare, banks, insurance, manufacturing, government) often hire straight from the fair, and they make great first internships. Many schools also post jobs on Handshake (a job site run through your career center). See [applying](applying.md#where-to-apply).

## Campus jobs

Tutor, lab assistant, IT help desk, or grader for a professor. They pay, they're flexible around classes, they go on your resume, and they put you next to faculty. A teaching assistant (TA) or tutor job also builds the skill of explaining code, which interviews test. (F-1 students: on-campus work has hour limits. Ask your DSO first.)

## References

Employers and programs sometimes ask for a reference: someone who will say good things about you.
- Ask a professor, TA, boss, or club adviser **before** you list them. Say: "Would you be willing to be a reference for internship applications? I can send my resume."
- Pick people who have seen your work, not only your grades.
- Tell them each time you apply somewhere that might call.

## Low-time version

If you work or commute and have about 5 hours a week, do only these:
- **Once this month:** one office-hours visit or one upperclassman message.
- **Once a week:** one alumni message (10 minutes).
- **Skip** clubs that meet at night unless one fits. Join their chat group and read it.
- **Take** one campus or online event a semester.

Even three contacts you keep warm make a difference.

## Resources

**Where to find people**
- [Handshake](https://joinhandshake.com): your school's job and career fair site. Ask your career center for access.
- [ACM](https://www.acm.org): student chapters, contests, and conferences for everyone in computing.
- [NSF REU sites](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/reu): paid summer research for undergraduates.

**Hackathons and contests**
- [Major League Hacking (MLH)](https://mlh.com) and [Devpost](https://devpost.com): hackathon calendars, in person and online.
- [ICPC](https://icpc.global): the big team programming contest.
- [Advent of Code](https://adventofcode.com): daily puzzles every December.
- Security contests (CTFs): [picoCTF](https://picoctf.org) for beginners. See [tracks](tracks.md).
