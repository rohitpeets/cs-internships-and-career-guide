# Government and Defense Jobs

Federal agencies, national labs, and defense companies hire students for software, cyber, data, embedded, and engineering work. Many of these jobs are overlooked by students who only watch big-tech postings. They can be easier to get into, and they often turn into full-time offers.

Rules, dates, and pay change often. Treat this guide as a map and confirm on each program's own page before you plan around it.

## Who hires

- **Federal agencies.** Examples: NASA, the Department of Energy, the Department of Defense and its branches. All are posted on [USAJOBS](https://www.usajobs.gov).
- **National labs.** Research labs run for the Department of Energy. Students usually apply through a program such as SULI (below).
- **Defense contractors.** Private companies that build things for the government, such as aircraft, satellites, and networks. They post on their own sites and on Handshake.
- **Consulting and IT firms** that serve government customers.

## Citizenship

Most of these jobs and programs need U.S. citizenship. Some accept permanent residents. Each posting says which, so read the "Who may apply" line first.

## What a clearance is

A **security clearance** is government approval to see classified information. Some jobs need one. Here is how it works in plain English:

- **You can't buy one or apply on your own.** An employer, such as an agency or a contractor, sponsors you. That usually happens after you get a job offer for a role that needs it.
- **It takes months.** Secret level often takes a few months. Top Secret can take much longer. Delays are common.
- **Interim clearance.** Sometimes you can start work with temporary access while the full check finishes. It is not automatic.
- **The form.** You fill out a long form (the SF-86) about the last 7 to 10 years: where you lived, jobs, travel, foreign contacts, money, and drug use. Then investigators check it.
- **Honesty matters most.** Leaving something out is usually worse than the thing itself. Lying on the form can end your chance. If you are unsure, ask the security officer at the employer, not a friend.
- **What can cause trouble:** large unpaid debts, drug use (including marijuana, which is still illegal under federal law, even where states allow it), criminal charges, and dishonesty.

This is general information, not legal advice. If you have a specific worry, ask the employer's security office or a lawyer.

**Tip:** a posting that says "ability to obtain a clearance" means you do not have one yet and the employer will sponsor you. A posting that says "active clearance required" is for people who already hold one. Students should skip those.

## Programs to know

Check each program's page for current dates and rules.

**USAJOBS Pathways Internship Program.** Paid federal internships for students who are enrolled at least half-time. Each agency runs its own. They can be part-time or full-time. If you finish the required hours (agencies often require at least 480), finish your degree, and do well, the agency can offer you a permanent job without a new competition. You must be a citizen by the end of the program to convert. Find them on [USAJOBS](https://www.usajobs.gov).

**DoD SMART Scholarship-for-Service.** The [SMART program](https://www.smartscholarship.org) pays full tuition and a yearly stipend. In return, you work for the Defense Department after you graduate, for as long as you were funded. It asks for a 3.0 GPA, and applications close in early December each year (the first Friday). Check the site for citizenship rules.

**CyberCorps Scholarship for Service.** The [SFS program](https://www.sfs.opm.gov) pays tuition, a yearly stipend, and a professional allowance for cybersecurity students for up to 3 years. Afterwards you work in government cybersecurity for as long as you were funded. You apply through a school that takes part, so check the SFS site for the list and ask your department.

**DOE national-lab internships (SULI).** [Science Undergraduate Laboratory Internships](https://science.osti.gov/wdts/suli) are paid terms at Department of Energy labs in summer, fall, and spring. You must be a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, have a 3.0 GPA, be a full-time student, and have finished at least one semester. Each term has its own deadline, posted months earlier. Community college students can look at the companion Community College Internships program.

**NASA internships.** NASA runs short project-based internships (apply through [NASA STEM Gateway](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/nasa-internship-programs)) and longer Pathways internships (apply on USAJOBS). You must be a U.S. citizen with at least a 2.9 GPA for Pathways, or a 3.0 GPA for the short internships. Deadlines come well ahead of each session: for example, the summer session closes around March 1.

Many agencies and labs have their own student programs too. Search their careers pages for "student" or "intern."

## When to apply

Dates differ by program. Here is a safe way to plan:

- **Fall:** check USAJOBS and defense company pages. Many summer roles open in the fall because clearances take months.
- **Winter:** NASA and DOE lab deadlines for summer often fall between winter and early spring.
- **December:** SMART scholarship deadline.
- **Spring:** keep checking. Some agencies hire late.

Put every deadline in your [tracker](applying.md). Government applications take longer than a normal one, so start at least a week before each deadline.

## How to search USAJOBS

1. Make a free account and fill in your profile. Save a resume.
2. Search for "intern" or "student trainee" or your field, such as "computer science" or "cybersecurity."
3. Open the **Hiring path** filter and choose **Students**. This shows jobs you may be eligible for.
4. Read the **Who may apply** and **Requirements** parts of each posting. Check for a citizenship rule and a GPA minimum.
5. Save the search and turn on email alerts.
6. Build your federal resume. It is often longer than a private-sector one, and it must show hours per week and dates for each job. Use the posting's wording, because a computer and a human resources specialist both screen it first.
